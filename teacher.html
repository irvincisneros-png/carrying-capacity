Teacher guide

Carrying Capacity

A browser game for Year 9 Science (NSW Science 7–10 syllabus, 2023, Environmental sustainability). Students grow a town through the industrial boom, see the climate and pollution consequences in their own graphs, then lead the transition while keeping people and wildlife safe.

Overview

Each scenario has two acts. In Act 1 (the boom) students build a settlement decade by decade from the 1800s to about 2020. The cheapest buildings are the dirtiest, so the damage feels earned rather than lectured. A turning point then lands (a fish kill, a drought, a bleaching event) and a scientist character puts the student's own graphs on the table. In Act 2 (the transition) time runs season by season to 2100: students replace dirty power, clean up pollution, restore land and water, deal with every old building through the waste hierarchy and keep the town's people on side through heatwaves, floods and fires.

The game is designed for empathy and care as much as content: named residents with families react to decisions, animals return or disappear, and dilemma cards have no single right answer. Every dilemma has a What does the evidence say? button, and the 2100 Report Card ends with reflection prompts.

Six places

A 20–30 minute tutorial and five scenarios from easy to expert, each about 70–120 minutes of play.

Real data

Graphs compare the student's CO₂ and temperature with the real record (Law Dome ice cores and Mauna Loa) and export as CSV.

Runs in a browser

No install or login. Tested on school laptops and Chromebooks; graphics drop to a lighter level automatically when needed.

Before the first lesson

  • Saves live in the browser. The game autosaves every in-game year, but only on that device and browser profile, and there is one save slot. Students who swap devices start again; clearing browsing data deletes the save.
  • Unlock order. Green Valley (tutorial) → Coal Coast → River Town and Reef Island → Red Dirt → Net Zero City. Use teacher mode to open any scenario straight away.
  • Same map, same weather. Every student gets the same map, and the same actions produce the same events, so results are comparable across the class.
  • Sound is on by default; headphones help. It can be muted in Settings.

How to run a lesson

Scenarios are longer than one lesson. Plan on two or three lessons per scenario, or set the second half as homework on the same device. The tutorial fits in one lesson.

60-minute lesson

  1. Hook (5 min). Show the Keeling curve or a photo of a fish kill. Ask: what would a town have to do to cause this?
  2. Set up (5 min). Students open the game. First-timers start Green Valley; everyone else continues their save.
  3. Play (35 min). Act 1 of Coal Coast to the turning point takes about 25 minutes. Students keep a one-line log of each decade's biggest decision.
  4. Graph talk (10 min). Pause at the turning point. Open Graphs (G): compare your CO₂ and temperature lines with the real ones. Why are they shaped the same?
  5. Exit ticket (5 min). "Name one building that made the biggest difference to your emissions and explain why."

90-minute lesson

  1. Hook and predictions (10 min). Students predict what will happen to the estuary, the bush and the air by 2020.
  2. Act 1 (25 min). Coal Coast to the turning point.
  3. Turning point discussion (10 min). Read the scientist's note together. Which of your choices show up in the graph?
  4. Act 2 (30 min). Start the transition: clean power first. Pause for every dilemma card and press What does the evidence say? before choosing.
  5. CSV task (10 min). Export the graph data (Graphs → CSV) and sketch emissions by decade.
  6. Reflection (5 min). One of the in-game reflection prompts in workbooks.

Assessment ideas

Graph analysis (4.2.2, 4.2.6)

At the turning point or the end, students open Graphs and press CSV. The file has one row per decade (Act 1) and per year (Act 2): their CO₂ and temperature, the real CO₂ and temperature to 2020, and emissions by sector (power, industry, transport, land, waste).

  • Plot their temperature against the real record. Describe the trend and explain any difference using their own build decisions.
  • Find the decade with the biggest jump in emissions. Which buildings caused it?
  • Calculate the percentage fall in yearly emissions from the start of Act 2 to 2050. Did warming fall too? Explain the lag.

Report Card write-up (4.5.1)

The 2100 Report Card shows each goal, the species census, the five decisions that mattered most and where each resident ended up. Students choose one pollution concern from their town (estuary runoff, smog over homes, contaminated soil, salt on farmland, light on a turtle beach) and write a case study:

  • the human activity that caused it, with evidence from their game;
  • its effects on people and on other living things;
  • what they did about it, what worked, what it cost and who it helped or hurt;
  • what a real community could learn from it.

Dilemma justification (4.1.2)

Pick one dilemma card. Record the options, the evidence, who each option helps and hurts, the student's choice and what happened next. Mark against how well the choice uses evidence and considers all three meters.

Reflection (not graded in game)

The ending shows two reflection prompts and a letter to the next generation. Answers are typed into the game but not saved or sent anywhere: ask students to copy them into workbooks or a document before closing the tab.

Reflection prompts used in the game

    Cultural safety

    All Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander content in this game is a draft. It has not yet been reviewed through the NSW AECG or a Local Aboriginal Land Council. Elder characters, place names, cultural burning, co-management and the mine-rehabilitation partnership are placeholders written to point towards respectful practice, and they are flagged in the code as needing consultation.

    What students see by default. Until that consultation is done, the public version of the game hides the draft content. There are no Elder characters or Elder story beats, no dual place names, and no draft Caring for Country text. The partnership, consulting about significant places, co-managing bush and planned burning still work as game mechanics, but they appear with neutral wording: Work with the local Aboriginal community (content being developed with community). The Acknowledgement of Country is always shown. A resident who is hidden does not affect the report card: the third star does not depend on a story students cannot see.

    Reviewing the draft. Add ?draft to the game's address (for example …/carrying-capacity/?draft, or ?teacher&draft to also unlock every scenario) to see the full draft content. A tag reading Draft — pending community consultation stays on screen so it cannot be mistaken for the finished game. Use this only with your Aboriginal Education Officer, AECG or community reviewers, not with students.

    • Before teaching with this content, speak with your school's Aboriginal Education Officer or local AECG, and use local resources and voices wherever possible.
    • Present Caring for Country as living knowledge held by communities and specific to each Country, not as a game mechanic. The game cannot teach it; it can only open a conversation.
    • The partnership in the game grows through asking, listening and returning land management, and cannot be bought. Use that as a discussion point.
    • Content warning: the game includes loss of homes in storms, heat deaths in Net Zero City, wildlife deaths and fish kills, described without graphic detail.

    Teacher mode

    Add ?teacher to the game's address (for example …/carrying-capacity/?teacher) or use the Open with every scenario unlocked button above. Every scenario is open on the scenario list for that visit, without the unlock order. It does not change saved games or earned stars, so students can use it too if you want them to jump straight to a scenario.

    Advanced: ?seed=1234 starts new games on a different map with different weather. Goals are balanced and tested on each scenario's own map, so use this only for curious groups who want to compare runs.

    Accessibility and graphics

    • Graphics (Settings): Auto, Low, Medium, High and Ultra. Auto measures the frame rate and steps down a level when the computer struggles; Low suits older Chromebooks.
    • Sound (Settings): on or off. No information is given by sound alone.
    • Keyboard: number keys pick buildings, R rotates, Q/E turn the view, X demolishes, Esc cancels or closes panels, G opens graphs, Ctrl+Z undoes the last placement and Space pauses Act 2. Buttons have labels for screen readers.
    • Pace: Act 2 can be paused at any time, and every dilemma card pauses the clock. Speeds are 1×, 2× and 4×.
    • Reading level: Year 9, Australian English. Science explanations sit behind "What does the evidence say?" and "The science" buttons, so students can go deeper when ready.
    • Phones and tablets: playable at 375 px wide in portrait (panels become bottom sheets), but a laptop is much more comfortable.
    • Motion: notifications respect the operating system's reduced-motion setting.

    Known limitations and simplifications

    Every model leaves things out. The same list is in the game under Settings → Simplified!, so students can check it themselves. Good discussion: "Which simplification matters most for the conclusions you drew?"

    Other things to know

    • Reflection answers and the letter are not saved or exported.
    • One save slot per browser; starting a new scenario replaces the save.
    • Prices, populations and emissions are game-scaled, not real figures for any real town.
    • Residents and places are fictional, inspired by real kinds of Australian towns (Illawarra, the Murray–Darling, Queensland islands, the Pilbara, a large coastal city).
