# Carrying Capacity: research facts for WP8 and WP9

Compiled 21 Oct 2026. Each figure has a source (name, year, URL). "FLAG" means uncertain, secondhand or my own calculation. Where I could only see a summary of a page (not the page itself), I say so.

## 1. Data centres in Australia (Sydney and NSW)

**Electricity now and forecast**
- About 165 data centres in the National Electricity Market (NEM) used about 5 TWh in 2025-26. That is about 3% of NEM electricity use. (AEMO, 2026 ESOO Data Centre Forecasting Overview, 2026, https://www.aemo.com.au/-/media/files/electricity/nem/planning_and_forecasting/nem_esoo/2026/2026-esoo-data-centre-forecasting-overview.pdf)
- Same source, Step Change scenario: about 15 TWh (7.8%) by 2029-30 and about 34 TWh (13%) by 2035-36, then growth slows.
- NSW had 66% of data centre demand in 2025-26, Victoria 30%. Average demand rose from nearly 200 MW (2018) to over 700 MW (2026). Same source.
- 225 projects with 67 GW were in the connection queue, but AEMO says 36% of projects in the 2025 ESOO were cancelled, and connections currently run at only about 27% of capacity. The queue is not a forecast.
- FLAG: a news report (w.media, https://w.media/aemo-data-centres-could-account-for-almost-10-of-nem-electricity-demand-by-2050/) says the 2026 ISP has 34 TWh (almost 10%) by 2050. That clashes with 34 TWh by 2036 above. I could not open the ISP. Use the ESOO numbers for the game.

**Size.** Existing sites are small: 700 MW over about 165 sites is roughly 4 MW each (FLAG: my calculation). New "hyperscale" campuses are huge: NEXTDC S7 at Eastern Creek, Western Sydney, is up to 612 MW (earlier 550 MW), first stage planned for late 2027 (DCD, https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/australian-stargate-data-center-in-sydney-will-offer-612mw-of-capacity/). CDC has an approved 504 MW site (The Urban Developer, 2025, https://www.theurbandeveloper.com/articles/eastern-creek-data-centre-550mw-openai-nextdc). Game suggestion: small = 5 MW, hyperscale = 500+ MW.

**Cooling water**
- Amazon says its Sydney region uses 0.10 litres per kWh (100 L per MWh), because outside air cools it 95.5% of the year. Melbourne is 0.06 L/kWh. (Amazon Australia, 25 Jun 2026, https://www.aboutamazon.com.au/news/aws/how-amazon-uses-water-to-keep-australias-digital-economy-running). Self-reported.
- Evaporative cooling is much thirstier. One industry benchmark is 1.8 L/kWh (1,800 L/MWh); a paper cites up to 2.5 (Preprints, 2026, https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202607.1593). FLAG: benchmarks vary.
- My calculation: a 1 MW data centre running all day uses 24,000 kWh, so about 43,000 L/day at 1.8 L/kWh versus about 2,400 L/day at 0.10.
- Sydney Water told a NSW inquiry that connection applications could mean 250 ML/day (250 million litres a day) by 2035. One application asked for 40 ML/day. Today data centres are about 0.7% of Sydney's supply. Sydney Water says that scenario is built on applications, not confirmed demand. (AAP, Jun 2026, https://aapnews.aap.com.au/news/ai-s-thirst-trap-tech-giant-taps-into-recycled-water; Univ. of Melbourne Pursuit, https://pursuit.unimelb.edu.au/articles/what-ai-data-centres-really-mean-for-australias-water-supply). FLAG: secondhand; I did not see the original submission.
- Western Sydney example: NEXTDC and OpenAI dropped a recycled-water plan for S7 because the pipeline had no planning permission, and chose closed-loop liquid cooling. That needs no drinking water but more electricity. (DCD report, https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/openai-data-center-in-sydney-australia-abandons-plans-for-recycled-water-cooling-system-report/). FLAG: I saw a summary only. A claimed NSW "PUE 1.3" limit is unconfirmed; the NSW framework text I found gave no number.

**Waste-heat reuse**
- Finland: Microsoft and Fortum plan data centres at Espoo feeding heat into the district heating network. The companies claim about 40% of local district heating and 400,000 t CO2 saved a year (ITPro, 2022, https://itpro.com/server-storage/data-centres/367088/microsoft-data-centre-to-heat-homes-and-businesses-in-finland). Company claims. Equinix Helsinki also supplies Helen's network, using heat pumps to lift the heat (IREI, https://irei.com/publications/article/microsoft-finnish-utility-capture-recycle-heat-data-centers/).
- Data centre heat is low-grade and needs heat pumps and pipes (RenewEconomy, https://reneweconomy.com.au/from-heat-pumps-to-pumped-heat-octopus-backs-tech-that-reuses-data-centre-heat/).
- UK: a pool in Exmouth cut heating bills by over 60% using a Deep Green "digital boiler" (same RenewEconomy article).
- Australia: I found no operating project. Canberra's Greens proposed putting data centres at public pools in Sept 2026; the Chief Minister rejected the siting but called heat reuse "worthwhile" (Region Canberra, 22 Sep 2026, https://region.com.au/barr-shoots-down-greens-call-for-data-centres-to-heat-pools/1001558/). Why so few: my reasoning (FLAG) is that Australia has almost no district heating.

**Honest benefits**
- Jobs: few and skilled once built. Microsoft's existing Australian sites employ about 200 people; its three planned builds need about 1,860 jobs at peak construction (Microsoft, 2025, https://local.microsoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Microsoft-datacentres-in-Australia.pdf). Only about 38% of spending stays in Australia, because chips and servers are imported (Oxford Economics, 2025, https://www.oxfordeconomics.com/resource/australias-data-centre-boom-three-lessons-for-capturing-economic-value/).
- Services: cloud, banking, health records, AI tools. (General; no figure.)
- Climate science: the Bureau of Meteorology and CSIRO run weather and climate models, including the ACCESS-Fire bushfire model, on the Gadi supercomputer at ANU (ANU, https://www.anu.edu.au/news/all-news/weather-and-climate-modelling-powers-up-at-anu). FLAG: Gadi is a research supercomputer, not a commercial data centre. I did not research AI-specific climate work.

**Conditions governments attach**
- NSW Data Centre Policy Framework (Aug 2026): six principles; a 75-day assessment if met; 100% recycled water or a committed shift to rain-independent water, with interim drinking-water use fully offset; power purchase agreements and firming for new NSW renewables; no diesel generators for load-shifting; apprenticeships and TAFE links. A proposed Bill covers connections of 5 MW or more. (Clayton Utz, Aug 2026, https://www.claytonutz.com/insights/2026/august/nsw-draws-the-line-what-the-new-data-centre-policy-framework-means-for-investors-developers-government-and-communities). Energy ministers are also considering requiring data centres to invest in renewables and demand flexibility (AEMO overview above).

**Candidate evidence lines (game card; each 45 words or fewer)**
1. Power: "Data centres used about 3% of grid electricity in 2026. AEMO expects 13% by 2035-36, and two-thirds of today's demand is in NSW. New wind, solar and storage must be built fast enough to match."
2. Water: "Cooling that evaporates water can use about 1,800 litres per megawatt-hour. Amazon reports about 100 in Sydney by using outside air. How a data centre is cooled matters more than how big it is."
3. Jobs: "Thousands of people help build a campus, but a running one needs a small, skilled crew. Microsoft's current Australian sites employ about 200 people. Most servers and chips are imported."
4. Conditions: "NSW's 2026 rules ask data centres to pay for new renewable power, use recycled water, and avoid diesel for load-shifting. In return, approval can take 75 days. Conditions share the cost."

## 2. Environmental innovations for the Act 1 timeline (1960-2020)

1. **Clean Air Act 1961 and Clean Waters Act 1970 (NSW).** The State Pollution Control Commission was created by a Dec 1970 Act and began 16 Jun 1971; it took over both Acts in 1974. It was replaced by a stronger EPA in 1991. Critique: it focused on "end-of-pipe" control. Benefit/cost: no figures found. (Research Data Australia, https://researchdata.edu.au/agy-1145-state-control-commission/164986; NSW Parliament Research, https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/parliamentary-business/research-and-library/research-publications-data/proposed-new-pollution-legislation-in-nsw-backgr). FLAG: the Clean Air Act is 1961, not the 1970s.
2. **Kerbside recycling.** Many councils began in the late 1980s and early 1990s; newspaper publishers helped pay. Orange (NSW) started in 1995. (Wikipedia, Recycling in Australia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recycling_in_Australia; Orange council). FLAG: weak sources, no cost figure. Check National Waste Reports before final text.
3. **Deep ocean outfalls, Sydney.** Malabar (Sep 1990), North Head (Dec 1990), Bondi (Aug 1991) replaced cliff-face outfalls that fouled beaches. Benefit: cleaner beaches. Critique: "moving the problem offshore"; Sydney Water says its sediment and species monitoring shows otherwise (Sustainability Matters, https://sustainabilitymatters.net.au/content/wastewater/article/history-of-wastewater-treatment-in-sydney-311574744; Sydney Water, 2025, https://www.sydneywater.com.au/content/dam/sydneywater/documents/deep-ocean-outfalls-assessment.pdf). FLAG: a 1991 Green Left article gives $106 million, but it is unclear whether that is one outfall or the whole program.
4. **Unleaded petrol and catalytic converters (1985-86).** Unleaded petrol arrived in 1985; new cars needed catalytic converters and unleaded fuel from 1986; leaded petrol was finally phased out on 1 Jan 2002, one of the longest phase-outs in the world (17 years). Benefit: the Australian Bureau of Statistics estimated 90% of urban air lead came from petrol; lead harms children's brains at low blood levels (NSW Public Health Bulletin, 1993, https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/phb/Documents/1993-11.pdf; LEAD Group, https://lead.org.au/fs/fst13.html). FLAG: advocacy sources; no cost figure.
5. **Ozone: CFC phase-out.** Montreal Protocol agreed 1987; Australia passed the Ozone Protection Act 1989; the government says Australia met or beat every phase-out target (Dept of Agriculture, Water and the Environment fact sheet, https://www.agriculture.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/ozone-fact-sheet.pdf; Parliamentary Library, 2018, https://aph.gov.au/About_Parliament/Parliamentary_Departments/Parliamentary_Library/FlagPost/2018/November/Montreal_Protocol). Recovery by about 2060-70 is attributed to CSIRO in a secondary source (FLAG). No cost-benefit study found.
6. **BASIX (NSW, 2004).** New homes must meet water and energy targets. Targets: 40% less water, 25% less greenhouse gas than 2004 homes; Sydney Water monitoring found savings of 40.6% (2008) and 37.6% (2009). Claimed total: 340 billion litres saved (Wikipedia BASIX, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BASIX; Architecture & Design, https://architectureanddesign.com.au/news/basix-saves-australian-homeowners-millions). FLAG: the 2004 start date is from memory; totals are industry-reported.
7. **NSW Solar Bonus Scheme (2010).** Paid 60 cents per kWh at the start; cut to 20 cents, then closed to new applicants in 2011 (28 Apr per the government). Cost forecast: up to $1.9 billion (2011 government) versus a "$2.4 billion deficit" claim. Lesson: great uptake, but the price was set too high. (NSW Government release, 2011, https://www.superiorsolar.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2011/05/130511-NSW-Govt-announces-closure-of-solar-bonus-scheme.pdf; pv magazine, https://www.pv-magazine.com/australia-solar-bonus-scheme-suspended-in-nsw). FLAG: two closure dates and two cost figures.
8. **Container deposit schemes.** South Australia 1977 (FLAG: from memory, not re-checked); NSW "Return and Earn" from Dec 2017. By Aug 2025 it passed 14 billion containers, with a claimed 73% drop in drink-container litter (Return and Earn, https://returnandearn.org.au/news/14-billion-drink-containers-recycled-through-return-and-earn). FLAG: operator figures; earlier releases said 44% (year 1) and 54%.

## 3. Water-quality "field kit" indicators

**Source for numbers.** The NSW Government environment page quotes the ANZECC/ARMCANZ (2000) default trigger values, which NSW still uses for the Australian and New Zealand Guidelines (ANZG 2018). The ANZG say site-specific values are preferred and the old defaults are guides, not pass/fail lines. (NSW Environment and Heritage, Indicators of Environmental Outcomes, https://www.environment.nsw.gov.au/ieo/CooksRiver/report-03.htm; ANZG overview, https://publications.water.nsw.gov.au/watergroupjspui/bitstream/100/87/1/Revised_Australian_and_New_Zealand_Guidelines_for_Fresh_and_Marine_Water_Quality_2018.pdf). FLAG: I could not open the ANZG 2018 tables themselves, so confirm there before print.

| Indicator | Lowland river (south-east Australia) | Upland river |
|---|---|---|
| Dissolved oxygen | 85-110% saturation | 90-110% |
| Total phosphorus (TP) | 25 ug/L (rivers to the coast); 50 ug/L (Murray-Darling) | 20 ug/L |
| Total nitrogen (TN) | 350 ug/L (coast); 500 ug/L (Murray-Darling) | 250 ug/L |
| Turbidity | 6-50 NTU | 2-25 NTU |
| pH | 6.5-8.5 | 6.5-8.0 |
| Electrical conductivity | 125-2,200 uS/cm | 30-350 uS/cm |
| Chlorophyll-a | 5 ug/L | n/a |

- Values were set from daytime readings; DO changes through the day. Higher turbidity is normal in high flows. Sandstone streams can naturally sit at pH 4.5 (same NSW page). Sydney's rivers drain to the coast, so use the coastal TP and TN.
- Dissolved oxygen in mg/L (FLAG: my calculation): fresh water at 20 C holds about 9.1 mg/L at 100% saturation, so 85-110% is about 7.7-10 mg/L. NSW EPA used 4 mg/L as a low-oxygen warning threshold in its 2024 Darling-Barka reporting (https://www.epa.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/quarterly-results-april-to-june-2024.pdf).
- Nitrate on its own: I did not confirm an ANZG trigger for oxidised nitrogen or ammonium. Use TN and TP in the game.
- Salinity: use conductivity (the 125-2,200 uS/cm range above); I found no separate salinity trigger.

**SIGNAL2 (macroinvertebrates).** Each bug group gets a sensitivity grade from 1 to 10 (10 = most sensitive to pollution). Score = total of grades / number of groups found (Waterwatch Victoria example; a weighted version counts how many were found). Grades (order-level table from the SIGNAL2 method, Chessman 2003, CSIRO https://publish.csiro.au/MF/MF02114; worked table at https://riverhealth.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Macroinvertebrates-Calculate-Alt-Signal-score.pdf):
- Sensitive: stonefly nymphs 10, mayfly nymphs 9, caddisfly larvae 8.
- Middle: mites 6, beetles 5, yabbies and shrimps 4.
- Tolerant: dragonfly/damselfly nymphs 3, true-fly larvae 3, proboscis worms 3, segmented worms 2, true bugs 2, flatworms 2.
- Families: snails Physidae 1 and Planorbidae 2; midges (Chironomidae, "bloodworms") 3-4. FLAG: I did not confirm a leech grade (often listed as 1).
- Bands: sensitive 10-8, 7-5, 4-3, tolerant 2-1 (Waterwatch macroinvertebrate survey guide, 2005, https://www.wirraminna.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/macroinvertebrate_survey_guide.pdf). Score meaning (Merri Creek Waterwatch report, https://mcmc.org.au/images/file/reports/Waterwatch_WQ_summaries/MCMC_Waterbug_Report_Merri_Ck_Fitzroy_YMR112_06022022.pdf): above 6 healthy; 5-6 mild pollution; 4-5 moderate; below 4 severe. Victorian bands; the report warns they suit "normal" streams.

## 4. Carrying capacity and ecological footprint

- **Earth Overshoot Day:** 30 July 2026, the "highest level of overshoot ever recorded" (Global Footprint Network, 5 Jun 2026, https://footprintnetwork.org/2026/06/05/announcement-earth-overshoot-day-2026-falls-on-july-30/). It was 24 July in 2025 (GFN, via https://www.socialjustice.ie/node/7244).
- **Australia's Overshoot Day** (if everyone lived like Australians): 19 March 2025 (Planet Ark, https://www.nationaltribune.com.au/australia-s-earth-overshoot-day-a-call-to-action-for-circular-economy-future-says-planet-ark/); 29 March 2026 (Sustainable Population Australia, https://newshub.medianet.com.au/2026/07/earth-overshoot-day-our-ecological-credit-card-is-maxed-out/163910/). FLAG: the 2026 date is from an advocacy group; Planet Ark's 2026 date not found.
- **Footprint versus biocapacity:** GFN (2020) put Australia's footprint at 160 million global hectares (gha) and biocapacity at 296 million gha; the 2019-20 Black Summer fires cut biocapacity to about 153 million gha, the first deficit (https://www.footprintnetwork.org/2020/08/07/2019-2020-australia-wildfires-biocapacity-deficit/). GFN's 2026 deficit list shows Australia with no deficit day: its biocapacity is 165% of its footprint (https://overshoot.footprintnetwork.org/country-deficit-days/).
- **Per person:** about 6.6 gha per Australian (WWF figure via University of Sydney, https://isa.sydney.edu.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/ISATBLInfo9_v1.pdf; matches 160 million gha divided by about 24 million people, FLAG: my calculation). World average: footprint 2.51 gha, biocapacity 1.48 gha per person (Wikipedia, citing 2025 results, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_ecological_footprint; FLAG: secondary). I could not retrieve GFN's latest Australian per-person figures; check data.footprintnetwork.org.
- **Why both are true:** Australia has lots of land for few people, so it is not in deficit at home. But a lifestyle of 6.6 gha is over four times the 1.48 gha each person on Earth has (my arithmetic).

**Kid-friendly definitions**
- *Carrying capacity:* the most living things a place can support for a long time with its food, water and space.
- *Overshoot:* using nature faster than it can regrow. Like spending more than you earn: it works for a while, but the savings run out.
- *Ecological footprint:* how much land and sea it takes to supply what we use and to absorb our waste. *Biocapacity:* how much nature can regrow in a year.

## 5. Hidden costs of clean tech (balanced)

- **Lithium:** Australia mined about 88,000 t (lithium content) in 2024, around 37% of the world's 240,000 t (USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026, reported secondhand; USGS page https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/lithium-statistics-and-information). FLAG: figures vary by article (82,700-92,000 t).
- **Greenbushes (WA):** a waste-rock and dam project sought clearing of up to 541 ha of native bushland; the 2019 expansion approval required offsets for habitat of black cockatoos, numbats and western ringtail possums, plus water controls for the Blackwood catchment (WA EPA, https://www.epa.wa.gov.au/sites/default/files/Extract_of_determination/Chair%20Determination%20-%20Greenbushes%20Lithium%20Mine%20Waste%20Rock%20Landforms%2C%20Salt%20Water%20Gully%20Dam%20and%20additional%20clearing%20for%20infrastructure.pdf). The proposed Andover project (Pilbara) would clear about 3,046 ha and dewater an orebody (WA EPA, https://epa.wa.gov.au/sites/default/files/Extract_of_determination/Chair%20Determination%20-%20Andover%20Lithium%20Project.pdf). A UTS paper says Australian hard-rock lithium has a higher global warming impact than Chile's brine (https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/rest/bitstreams/070351ce-7de8-4aff-b37e-1bbc5d87338a/retrieve). FLAG: I found little independent data on mine water use or Aboriginal heritage.
- **Rare earths:** Lynas, an Australian company, refines in Malaysia, leaving low-level radioactive residue stored on site; a study for a Malaysian NGO says leachate can reach groundwater (Oeko-Institut, https://www.oeko.de/en/news/press-releases/rare-earth-refining-in-malaysia-without-coherent-waste-management-concept/). FLAG: licence ran to March 2026; current status not checked.
- **Solar panel end of life:** UNSW (ACAP) scoping study, 2024: up to 100,000 t a year by about 2030; about 1 million tonnes in total by 2035 (rooftop about 892,000 t, large farms about 265,000 t) (pv magazine Australia, 28 Mar 2024, http://www.pv-magazine-australia.com/2024/03/28/study-stresses-urgent-need-for-action-with-solar-waste-tipped-to-reach-1-2-gw-annually/). Landfill costs about $2 a panel; recycling $10-20. NSW alone: 34,000-63,000 t a year by 2035 (NSW EPA Circular Solar guidelines, https://epa.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/21p3135-circular-solar-phase-2-grants-application-guidelines.pdf). FLAG: totals differ slightly by report (1.157 million t cited by the Clean Energy Council).
- **Battery recycling:** CSIRO (2018): only about 2% of 3,300 t of lithium-ion battery waste was recycled; waste growing 20% a year, possibly over 100,000 t by 2036; lead-acid batteries 98% recycled; recoverable value $813 million-$3 billion (https://www.australianmanufacturing.com.au/australia-has-potential-to-spearhead-global-lithium-ion-battery-recycling-revolution-csiro-report-finds/). FLAG: 2018 data; I found no current national rate.
- **Balance (framing, not sourced here):** these costs are real and fixable, since lead-acid proves high battery recycling is possible. Panels and batteries can be recycled and mines rehabilitated; fossil fuels cannot be "recycled" after burning.
